# 1. Introduction

## 1. Introduction

Flooding remains one of the most pervasive and destructive natural hazards globally, with its frequency, intensity, and societal impact projected to increase due to anthropogenic climate change, rapid urbanization, and expanding human settlements into vulnerable floodplains [Jafarzadegan et al. 2023; Dottori et al. 2016]. Extreme precipitation events, coupled with alterations in land use, have substantially amplified hydrological risk across diverse geographical regions. In Eastern Europe, and particularly in Ukraine, this risk is further exacerbated by complex geomorphological settings, transboundary river systems such as the Prut and Dniester, and recent large-scale hydrological disasters. Notable events include the 2020 Carpathian floods, which caused widespread damage, and the catastrophic 2023 Kakhovka Dam breach, which resulted in the rapid inundation of vast territories (Figure F8) [Yailymov et al. 2025]. These incidents underscore the critical need for rapid, spatially explicit, and operationally reliable flood inundation mapping systems to support emergency response, disaster management, and post-disaster recovery, especially under conditions of high uncertainty and compromised infrastructure.

Accurate and timely flood mapping is indispensable for effective evacuation planning, efficient allocation of emergency logistics, comprehensive post-disaster damage assessment, and robust hydrological risk analysis [Schumann et al. 2018; Klemas 2015]. Traditional ground-based monitoring systems, primarily relying on hydrological gauging stations, offer high temporal resolution but are inherently limited by their sparse spatial coverage. Such point-based observations are often insufficient to capture the complex, heterogeneous dynamics of floodplain inundation and may become inoperable during extreme events due to infrastructure failure, communication disruptions, or restricted field accessibility [Wu et al. 2012].

Satellite remote sensing has emerged as a pivotal alternative, providing synoptic and spatially continuous observations of flood extents over extensive geographic areas [Anusha & Bharathi 2020]. Optical sensors, such as those aboard the Sentinel-2 and Landsat missions, detect inundation based on distinct spectral reflectance characteristics, typically employing water-related indices like the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) [Xu 2006; Huang et al. 2018]. Under optimal, cloud-free conditions, MNDWI-based approaches frequently achieve high Overall Accuracy (OA) values, often exceeding 93% in agricultural and mixed land-cover environments [Zhang et al. 2025]. This performance is notably higher than the corpus-wide median OA of 89.7% for flood mapping studies (NumericFact Statistics: OA median 89.7%, P75 94.0%). However, optical flood mapping is fundamentally constrained by atmospheric conditions, particularly cloud cover, and illumination, which frequently coincide with peak flood events, severely limiting their operational utility [Ekeu-wei & Blackburn 2018].

In contrast, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) systems, notably the Sentinel-1 constellation, offer all-weather and day-and-night imaging capabilities, enabling near-real-time (NRT) flood detection based on radar backscatter characteristics [Schumann & Moller 2015; Amitrano et al. 2024]. This inherent robustness to atmospheric conditions has led to the widespread adoption of SAR-based systems in operational flood services, such as the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS) [Wania et al. 2021]. Automated Sentinel-1 workflows can deliver initial flood products within hours after acquisition, with reported latencies ranging from 10 minutes to 24 hours, significantly enhancing situational awareness during rapidly evolving flood events [Twele et al. 2016; Vanama et al. 2020]. For instance, CEMS SAR products achieve a median delivery time of 6.4 hours, compared to 39.5 hours for optical products, although integration with platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) can introduce additional ingestion delays of 30–70 hours [Wania et al. 2021]. Despite these advantages, SAR imagery can suffer from classification errors in densely vegetated floodplains, with omission rates reaching up to 36%, and false positives in urban areas due to double-bounce effects [Giustarini et al. 2013; Liang & Liu 2020; Islam & Meng 2022].

Recent advancements in Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) have substantially improved the performance and automation of satellite-based flood mapping [Bentivoglio et al. 2022; Amer 2024]. Supervised algorithms, such as Random Forest, demonstrate strong classification accuracy when applied to multi-polarization SAR imagery (e.g., Sentinel-1 VH/VV), achieving Overall Accuracies exceeding 94% with Cohen's Kappa values between 0.75 and 0.80 [Islam & Meng 2022; Toma et al. 2024]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), particularly U-Net architectures, have further advanced automated high-resolution flood delineation through semantic segmentation [Nemni et al. 2020; Andrew et al. 2023]. While reported studies indicate that advanced DL models can achieve F1-scores approaching 0.98 and OA up to 99% under optimized conditions, corpus-wide distributions suggest that such values represent the upper extreme of reported flood segmentation accuracy (NumericFact Statistics: F1 median 80.0%, P75 90.0%; OA median 89.7%, P75 94.0%). The temporal evolution of methods, as illustrated in Figure F4, clearly shows the increasing prominence of deep learning approaches since 2018, alongside the sustained use of Random Forest and hydrological terrain models like HAND.

The integration of multi-source datasets, including SAR, optical imagery, and Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), further enhances mapping reliability and operational robustness [Destefanis et al. 2025; Revilla-Romero et al. 2015]. Topographic indices, such as Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND), provide crucial geomorphological constraints by incorporating terrain information into flood detection workflows, improving accuracy, particularly in complex topographies [Rennó et al. 2008; Guo et al. 2025]. Multi-sensor integration has thus become a central direction in operational flood mapping architectures, combining the spectral sensitivity of optical imagery, the weather independence of SAR, and the hydrological context provided by DEM-derived terrain models [Kuntla 2020].

Despite these technological advances, a fundamental challenge persists: the inherent trade-off between mapping accuracy, processing timeliness, transferability across diverse environments, and operational scalability. Highly accurate approaches frequently necessitate extensive preprocessing, large annotated datasets for training, or computationally intensive deep learning architectures, which can increase operational latency. Conversely, rapid automated systems prioritize timely situational awareness but may introduce classification uncertainties, particularly in challenging environments such as urban areas, densely vegetated floodplains, or mountainous terrain [Gašparovič & Klobučar 2021]. This balance between accuracy and timeliness is especially critical for operational disaster response systems, where delays of several hours can substantially diminish the practical value of flood information [Wania et al. 2021].

Eastern Europe and Ukraine provide particularly salient test environments for evaluating such operational flood mapping systems. Recent flood events in the Prut River basin have demonstrated that Sentinel-1-based approaches can achieve high spatial agreement with reference flood products, even in transboundary regions characterized by fragmented hydrological observations (Figure F8) [Cîmpianu et al. 2021]. Similarly, the Kakhovka Dam disaster emphasized the critical importance of rapid satellite-based flood assessment under conditions of damaged infrastructure, limited field accessibility, and large-scale environmental disruption, where an estimated 473 km² of land was inundated with an OA of 90.4% (Figure F8) [Yailymov et al. 2025].

Therefore, this review aims to critically evaluate the balance between accuracy and timeliness in satellite-based flood mapping approaches, with a specific focus on their applicability and performance in data-constrained and high-risk environments such as Ukraine. The objectives of this study are to: (i) compare the performance characteristics and limitations of optical, SAR-based, and machine learning flood mapping methodologies; (ii) assess their temporal efficiency and operational latency in the context of near-real-time disaster response; and (iii) identify robust strategies for integrating multi-sensor approaches into practical operational flood monitoring frameworks.

## 2. Optical Flood Mapping

Optical remote sensing offers a foundational approach to flood inundation mapping by leveraging the distinct spectral reflectance properties of water bodies. Floodwaters typically exhibit strong absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) and shortwave infrared (SWIR) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, coupled with relatively higher reflectance in the visible green band [Huang et al. 2018]. This spectral contrast enables the delineation of inundated areas through the application of various water indices.

Among these, the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI), introduced by [Xu 2006], is widely recognized for its effectiveness in enhancing open water features and suppressing noise from built-up areas and vegetation. MNDWI utilizes the green and SWIR bands, calculated as (Green - SWIR) / (Green + SWIR), which has been shown to outperform its predecessor, the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), particularly in environments with complex land cover [Zhang et al. 2025]. Studies employing MNDWI with high-resolution optical data from missions such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat have frequently reported Overall Accuracy (OA) values exceeding 93% under optimal clear-sky conditions, especially in agricultural and mixed land-cover environments [Huang et al. 2018; Cîmpianu et al. 2021]. This performance is notably higher than the typical OA values reported for NDWI, which often remain below 90% [Zhang et al. 2025].

While such high accuracies are achievable, it is crucial to contextualize these figures within the broader landscape of remote sensing flood mapping performance. As illustrated in Figure F1, the corpus-wide distribution of Overall Accuracy (OA) for flood mapping studies indicates a median OA of 89.7% and a 75th percentile of 94.0%. This suggests that MNDWI's reported performance exceeding 93% represents the upper quartile of typical flood mapping accuracy, often achieved under favorable conditions and optimized thresholds.

Despite its high accuracy under ideal circumstances, the fundamental limitation of optical remote sensing for flood monitoring is its susceptibility to atmospheric conditions. Cloud cover, prevalent during many extreme precipitation events that trigger floods, obscures the land surface, rendering optical sensors ineffective [Klemas 2015]. Furthermore, optical imagery relies on solar illumination, restricting observations to daytime hours. These constraints severely limit the operational timeliness and applicability of optical methods for near-real-time (NRT) flood response, as critical information may be unavailable precisely when it is most needed [Anusha & Bharathi 2020].

The integration of optical data, such as from Sentinel-2, can complement other sensor types in multi-sensor frameworks, particularly when clear-sky conditions permit. For instance, co-occurrence analysis of flood mapping methods (Figure F3) reveals that optical methods are often paired with Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) in comprehensive approaches, leveraging their respective strengths to overcome individual limitations [Nhangumbe et al. 2023; Destefanis et al. 2025]. However, for rapid, all-weather, and day-and-night monitoring capabilities, alternative remote sensing paradigms are essential.

# 3. SAR-Based Flood Mapping

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) has emerged as a cornerstone technology for operational flood mapping due to its inherent capability to acquire imagery irrespective of atmospheric conditions (clouds, rain) and illumination (day or night) [Schumann & Moller 2015; Twele et al. 2016]. This all-weather, day-and-night imaging capacity is particularly critical for rapid response during flood events, where optical sensors are often hindered by persistent cloud cover. The Sentinel-1 constellation, comprising two C-band SAR satellites, provides frequent global coverage, enabling timely flood detection and monitoring with revisit times as low as six days, or even less in specific regions [Kuntla 2020].

The fundamental principle of SAR-based flood detection relies on the distinct interaction of radar microwaves with water surfaces compared to land. Smooth water bodies act as specular reflectors, causing the radar signal to bounce away from the sensor, resulting in significantly reduced backscatter intensity (appearing as dark tones) in SAR imagery [Schumann & Moller 2015]. Conversely, rough surfaces, such as vegetated areas, bare soil, or urban infrastructure, scatter the radar signal in multiple directions, leading to higher backscatter (brighter tones). This pronounced contrast facilitates the delineation of inundated areas.

Common methodologies for extracting flood extent from SAR imagery include thresholding, region growing, and supervised classification [Chini et al. 2017; Pulvirenti et al. 2011]. Supervised machine learning algorithms, particularly Random Forest (RF), have demonstrated robust performance in flood mapping applications using Sentinel-1 dual-polarization (VH/VV) data [Islam & Meng 2022; Toma et al. 2024]. Studies employing RF on Sentinel-1 VH/VV polarizations frequently report Overall Accuracy (OA) values exceeding 94%, with Cohen's kappa coefficients typically ranging from 0.75 to 0.80 [Islam & Meng 2022; Toma et al. 2024]. While these figures represent strong performance, it is important to contextualize them within the broader literature. As illustrated in Figure 1, an OA of 94% aligns with the 75th percentile of reported overall accuracies across the NumericFacts corpus (median OA: 89.7%, P75: 94.0%), indicating that such performance is at the upper end of typical flood mapping results. Similarly, kappa values in the 0.75–0.80 range are slightly below the corpus median (83.7%) but within the lower quartile (P25: 70.5%), suggesting variability in agreement metrics depending on the specific flood event and environmental conditions.

To enhance classification consistency and mitigate false positives, multi-temporal SAR approaches are increasingly employed [Anusha & Bharathi 2020; Nhangumbe et al. 2023]. By comparing pre-flood and during-flood SAR acquisitions, these methods leverage temporal changes in backscatter, effectively distinguishing permanent water bodies from transient floodwaters and reducing noise from static features. In operational European applications, multi-temporal SAR techniques have further improved accuracy, achieving Overall Accuracy values exceeding 96% and Kappa coefficients above 0.78 [Toma et al. 2024]. The co-occurrence heatmap in Figure 3 highlights the strong association between SAR and traditional machine learning methods like Random Forest, reflecting their widespread use in the field.

A significant advantage of SAR is its capability for near-real-time (NRT) flood mapping. Sentinel-1 data can be processed rapidly, enabling the delivery of flood maps within 10 minutes to 24 hours of acquisition [Twele et al. 2016]. Operational services, such as the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS), leverage SAR data to provide rapid flood extent products, with reported delivery times averaging 6.4 hours, significantly faster than typical optical-based products which can take up to 39.5 hours [Wania et al. 2021]. However, the integration of SAR data into cloud-based platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) can introduce additional ingestion delays, ranging from 30 to 70 hours, which must be considered for truly time-critical applications [Vanama et al. 2020].

Despite these advantages, SAR-based flood mapping is not without limitations. Dense vegetation, such as forests or agricultural crops, can attenuate the radar signal, leading to significant omission errors where floodwaters beneath the canopy are undetected [Giustarini et al. 2013; Gašparovič & Klobučar 2021]. In such densely vegetated floodplains, omission errors can reach up to 36% [Giustarini et al. 2013]. Conversely, wind-induced surface roughness on open water can increase backscatter, causing false negatives, while geometric distortions like layover and shadowing, particularly in mountainous terrain and dense urban environments, can lead to misclassifications [Liang & Liu 2020; Islam & Meng 2022]. The "double-bounce" effect in urban areas, where the radar signal reflects off a vertical structure (e.g., building wall) and then a horizontal surface (e.g., street) back to the sensor, can mimic water surfaces, resulting in false positives [Giustarini et al. 2013]. These challenges necessitate the integration of ancillary data, such as Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) and land cover maps, to refine flood delineations and reduce uncertainty in complex environments.

# 4. Machine Learning and Deep Learning Approaches

## 4. Machine Learning and Deep Learning Approaches

The increasing availability of multi-source satellite imagery has catalyzed a significant paradigm shift in flood mapping, moving from traditional threshold-based methods towards advanced machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms [Bentivoglio et al. 2022; Nemni et al. 2020]. These data-driven approaches automate complex feature extraction and enhance classification performance across diverse and heterogeneous environments. Algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), including U-Net architectures, have demonstrated superior capabilities in learning intricate spatial and spectral relationships from multi-temporal and multi-sensor datasets [Amer 2024; Bentivoglio et al. 2022].

Deep learning models, especially U-Net architectures, have emerged as a leading methodology for semantic flood segmentation due to their ability to process high-resolution imagery and capture fine-scale inundation patterns. Studies have reported exceptional performance metrics, with U-Net models achieving overall accuracy (OA) values approaching 99% and F1-scores up to 0.98 for SAR flood segmentation [Nemni et al. 2020; Andrew et al. 2023]. For instance, Intersection over Union (IoU) values have been documented to exceed 0.8 for Sentinel-1 SAR data and 0.9 for Sentinel-2 optical imagery in specific benchmark scenarios [Nemni et al. 2020; Andrew et al. 2023]. While these peak performances highlight the potential of DL, it is crucial to contextualize them within broader empirical distributions. Corpus-wide analysis of flood mapping studies reveals that while such high F1 scores (0.98) represent the upper extreme, the median F1 score is approximately 80.0%, with the 75th percentile at 90.0% (Figure F1, Figure F6). Similarly, reported IoU values, while reaching above 0.9, are situated against a corpus median of 77.7% and a 75th percentile of 87.4% (Figure F1, Figure F6). This indicates that while DL can achieve state-of-the-art results, consistent high performance across diverse conditions remains a research challenge. The temporal evolution of methods, as depicted in Figure F4, clearly illustrates the rapid emergence and sustained growth of deep learning techniques since 2018, alongside the continued relevance of Random Forest and topographic methods like HAND.

Despite their impressive performance, advanced ML and DL models present several operational and methodological challenges. They typically require extensive, high-quality labeled training datasets, which are often scarce for specific flood events or geographic regions [Bentivoglio et al. 2022]. Furthermore, the computational demands for training and inference, coupled with the need for careful hyperparameter optimization, can be substantial. A critical limitation is the reduced generalizability of DL flood models, particularly U-Net architectures, when applied to geographically distinct regions or previously unseen flood events [Bentivoglio et al. 2022; Nemni et al. 2020]. This issue of transferability can hinder their operational robustness and necessitates domain adaptation strategies or continuous model retraining for new environments.

To mitigate the limitations inherent in single-sensor methodologies and enhance model generalizability, multi-source data fusion has become a cornerstone of modern flood mapping systems [Destefanis et al. 2025; Anusha & Bharathi 2020]. The integration of complementary information from SAR, optical imagery, and topographic data significantly improves classification reliability. Topographic information, derived from Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), is particularly valuable. Indices such as Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND) provide crucial terrain constraints, enabling the identification of hydraulically connected low-relief floodplain areas and reducing physically implausible inundation patterns [Rennó et al. 2008; Revilla-Romero et al. 2015]. This fusion approach leverages the strengths of each data type: SAR's all-weather capability, optical imagery's detailed spectral information, and DEMs' hydrological context, leading to more robust and accurate flood extent delineation [Destefanis et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025].

Figure F1 visually represents these integration pathways, illustrating the transition from isolated single-sensor analyses towards sophisticated multi-source operational architectures. These integrated frameworks combine remote sensing observations, advanced terrain analysis, and data-driven classification methods, moving beyond simple thresholding to adaptive systems capable of handling complex environmental variability [Destefanis et al. 2025]. While cloud-based platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) facilitate the processing of these multi-source datasets, operational processing delays, typically ranging from 30 to 70 hours from satellite acquisition to actionable output, can still limit their utility for strict near-real-time (NRT) applications [Vanama et al. 2020; Twele et al. 2016]. Nevertheless, the adoption of such hybrid architectures provides improved robustness, enhanced spatial consistency, and greater adaptability across diverse hydrological and environmental conditions, supporting both rapid emergency response and high-accuracy post-event flood assessment [Islam & Meng 2022; Mateo-Garcia et al. 2021].

The quantitative assessment of satellite-based flood mapping approaches is critical for evaluating their reliability, spatial agreement, and robustness across diverse environmental and operational scenarios. Accuracy metrics commonly employed include Overall Accuracy (OA), F1-score, Intersection over Union (IoU), and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. These metrics collectively characterize classification performance, with OA providing a general measure of correctly classified pixels, F1-score balancing precision and recall, IoU quantifying the spatial overlap between predicted and ground truth flood extents, and kappa accounting for agreement by chance [Dash & Sar 2020]. However, the reported accuracy of flood mapping varies substantially, influenced by sensor characteristics, methodological design, environmental conditions, preprocessing workflows, and the quality of reference data [Klemas 2015].

Optical remote sensing approaches, particularly those leveraging spectral indices like the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI), demonstrate high performance under optimal atmospheric conditions. Empirical studies indicate that MNDWI frequently achieves Overall Accuracy values exceeding 93% in mixed land-cover environments, often outperforming traditional Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) methods that typically remain below 90% OA [Xu 2006; Zhang et al. 2025]. This spectral sensitivity allows for detailed discrimination between flooded and non-flooded surfaces, especially in agricultural and low-vegetation regions. However, the operational reliability of optical flood mapping rapidly diminishes under cloud cover, atmospheric scattering, haze, and low illumination, conditions that frequently coincide with peak flood events [Huang et al. 2018]. Consequently, the high accuracies reported for optical methods are often conditional on clear-sky acquisition scenarios, limiting their utility for near-real-time response during active flood events.

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)-based approaches offer more robust and operationally consistent performance across varying weather conditions, including cloud cover and darkness [Schumann & Moller 2015; Amitrano et al. 2024]. Supervised machine learning methods, such as Random Forest classifiers applied to Sentinel-1 VH/VV backscatter data, frequently achieve OA values exceeding 94%, with Cohen’s kappa coefficients ranging between 0.75 and 0.80 [Islam & Meng 2022]. More advanced multi-temporal SAR frameworks further enhance classification consistency by incorporating temporal backscatter dynamics, leading to reported OA values exceeding 96% and kappa coefficients above 0.78 in operational European applications [Toma et al. 2024; Wania et al. 2021]. Despite these advancements, SAR-based flood mapping exhibits considerable performance variability, with OA values ranging from approximately 79% to 98% and F1-scores typically between 0.74 and 0.96 across the literature. This variability is particularly pronounced in complex environments such as urban areas and densely vegetated floodplains, where geometric distortions, canopy attenuation, and double-bounce scattering effects introduce additional uncertainty [Giustarini et al. 2013; Gašparovič & Klobučar 2021].

Deep learning (DL) approaches currently represent the state-of-the-art for pixel-level flood inundation delineation, particularly in complex flood environments. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architectures, such as U-Net-based encoder–decoder models, consistently outperform traditional thresholding and classical machine learning methods [Bentivoglio et al. 2022; Amer 2024]. Reported studies demonstrate very high pixel-level classification agreement under optimized experimental conditions, with some studies reporting OA values approaching 99% and F1-scores reaching 0.98 [Nemni et al. 2020]. Similarly, advanced semantic segmentation frameworks frequently achieve IoU values exceeding 0.8 for Sentinel-1 data and 0.9 for Sentinel-2 imagery [Andrew et al. 2023].

However, it is crucial to contextualize these peak performance figures against corpus-wide metric distributions. As illustrated in Figure 1 and Figure 6, while deep learning methods can achieve exceptional results, such values often represent the upper extreme of reported performance. The NumericFacts corpus indicates that the median F1-score across all flood mapping studies is 80.0% (P75: 90.0%), the median IoU is 77.7% (P75: 87.4%), and the median OA is 89.7% (P75: 94.0%). This suggests that while DL models hold significant promise, their reported high accuracies are often obtained under specific, optimized experimental conditions and may not always be representative of generalized operational performance. Furthermore, deep learning models often exhibit reduced transferability when applied to geographically distinct regions or previously unseen flood events, limiting operational generalization and requiring extensive region-specific training datasets [Bentivoglio et al. 2022].

Topographic information also plays a critical role in controlling flood mapping accuracy and spatial consistency, especially for refining flood boundaries and estimating flood depths [Cohen et al. 2018; Peter et al. 2020]. High-resolution Digital Terrain Models (DTMs) derived from airborne LiDAR achieve vertical errors as low as 0.05–0.20 m, substantially improving flood boundary delineation [Hawker et al. 2022]. In contrast, global DEM products such as SRTM and ASTER introduce considerably larger uncertainties, with vertical errors typically ranging between 16 and 20 m [Yamazaki et al. 2017]. This terrain-related uncertainty propagates directly into flood extent delineation, particularly in low-relief floodplains where small elevation errors may substantially alter hydraulic connectivity and inundation boundaries [Rennó et al. 2008].

Overall, the analysis indicates that no single methodology consistently maximizes flood mapping accuracy across all environmental and operational scenarios. Instead, mapping performance emerges from the complex interaction between sensor physics, environmental complexity, preprocessing quality, terrain representation, and model generalization capability. # 6. Operational Timeliness and Near-Real-Time Systems

## 6. Operational Timeliness and Near-Real-Time Systems

The efficacy of flood mapping in disaster response is critically dependent on operational timeliness, as the value of spatial information diminishes rapidly with increasing latency during fast-evolving flood events. The temporal performance of satellite-based systems is governed by three primary factors: image acquisition latency, satellite revisit frequency, and subsequent processing and dissemination delays.

Among Earth Observation (EO) sensors, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) systems, particularly Sentinel-1, offer the most robust near-real-time (NRT) capability due to their all-weather, day-and-night imaging capacity [Twele et al. 2016; Wania et al. 2021; Amitrano et al. 2024]. Sentinel-1 data can be disseminated within 10 minutes to 24 hours post-acquisition, enabling rapid initial assessments [Twele et al. 2016]. This contrasts sharply with optical systems, such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat, which are fundamentally constrained by atmospheric conditions. Cloud cover, prevalent during many flood events, can significantly delay the acquisition of usable optical data, often by more than 39 hours, potentially missing peak inundation phases [Zhang et al. 2025]. Operational frameworks, such as the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS), quantitatively underscore this disparity, reporting an average time of approximately 6.4 hours for the first usable SAR image acquisition compared to 39.5 hours for optical systems [Wania et al. 2021].

However, the journey from raw satellite acquisition to actionable flood extent maps is subject to additional processing delays. While Sentinel-1 data are rapidly distributed by the European Space Agency (ESA), their ingestion into cloud-based processing platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) can introduce a substantial latency of 30 to 70 hours [Vanama et al. 2020; Islam & Meng 2022]. This significant delay decouples the satellite acquisition from the actionable output, thereby limiting the feasibility of strict NRT applications that demand sub-hourly or immediate information delivery. The multi-sensor data flow and processing pathways, including potential bottlenecks at cloud platform ingestion, are conceptually illustrated in Figure 7.

Beyond acquisition and processing, satellite revisit frequency imposes a fundamental constraint on temporal resolution. Sentinel-1 offers a nominal 6-day repeat cycle, reducible to 3 days with orbit overlap, while Sentinel-2 provides 5-day coverage, and Landsat operates at 16-day intervals [Kuntla 2020]. These intervals mean that even with rapid processing, short-duration flash floods or rapidly evolving inundation events may be missed between successive passes, highlighting a critical limitation for comprehensive NRT monitoring.

Operational systems exhibit varying trade-offs between timeliness, accuracy, and processing complexity, as visually represented by the Pareto frontier in Figure 2/F10. Fully automated SAR-based workflows, often employing thresholding or machine learning techniques, can generate flood extent maps within minutes to less than 12 hours after data availability [Twele et al. 2016; Wania et al. 2021; Toma et al. 2024]. These systems are crucial for immediate situational awareness and emergency response, prioritizing speed for rapid decision-making. In contrast, semi-automated systems, such as those employed by UNITAR-UNOSAT, typically require 24 to 72 hours for product delivery due to the integration of human-in-the-loop validation, which prioritizes enhanced accuracy and reliability over raw speed [Vanama et al. 2020].

An alternative approach to satellite-based observations involves hydrological modeling systems. For instance, the NASA Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS) bypasses satellite acquisition delays by utilizing precipitation-driven simulations, delivering updates every 3 hours [Wu et al. 2012]. While offering continuous NRT information, these model-based outputs typically feature coarser spatial resolutions (~1 km), which may be insufficient for detailed local-scale impact assessment.

To further reduce end-to-end latency, advanced operational strategies integrate predictive and observational frameworks. Pre-tasking satellite acquisitions using early warning systems, such as the European Flood Awareness System (EFAS) within CEMS, can reduce response times by approximately half a day [Wania et al. 2021]. Looking forward, the integration of edge computing and in-orbit machine learning processing holds significant promise for reducing latency towards true NRT flood monitoring, potentially enabling actionable flood maps within minutes of data acquisition [Wania et al. 2021; Twele et al. 2016]. Such innovations are critical for bridging the gap between rapid data acquisition and the timely delivery of actionable intelligence for disaster management.

# 7. Accuracy versus Timeliness: The Operational Trade-off

## 7. Accuracy versus Timeliness: The Operational Trade-off

The operational utility of flood mapping is intrinsically linked to its timeliness, as the value of spatial inundation information diminishes rapidly with increasing latency during fast-evolving flood events. The temporal performance of satellite-based systems is governed by three primary components: image acquisition latency, satellite revisit frequency, and subsequent data processing and dissemination delays. Achieving a balance between high accuracy and rapid delivery constitutes a critical trade-off in operational flood response, as illustrated by the Pareto frontier in Figure 2/F10, which plots overall accuracy against operational latency.

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) systems, particularly Sentinel-1, offer the most robust near-real-time (NRT) capability due to their all-weather, day-and-night imaging capacity [Amitrano et al. 2024; Schumann & Moller 2015]. Sentinel-1 data can be disseminated within 10 minutes to 24 hours post-acquisition, enabling rapid flood mapping [Twele et al. 2016; Wania et al. 2021]. Operational frameworks, such as the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS), demonstrate this advantage, reporting an average time of approximately 6.4 hours to acquire the first usable SAR image for flood events. In stark contrast, optical systems like Sentinel-2 and Landsat are fundamentally constrained by atmospheric conditions, with cloud cover frequently delaying usable data acquisition by more than 39 hours, potentially missing peak inundation phases [Zhang et al. 2025]. This significant difference in acquisition latency is a primary driver of the operational trade-off.

However, even with rapid SAR acquisition, subsequent processing infrastructure introduces additional delays. For instance, while raw Sentinel-1 data are quickly distributed by ESA, ingestion into cloud-based platforms such as Google Earth Engine (GEE) can add a latency of 30 to 70 hours [Vanama et al. 2020], effectively decoupling the satellite acquisition from actionable product generation and limiting strict NRT applications (Thesis T8, CCS: MEDIUM). This highlights a critical distinction between raw data availability and processed, usable flood maps. Revisit frequency further constrains temporal resolution, with Sentinel-1 providing a nominal 6-day repeat cycle (reduced to 3 days with orbit overlap), Sentinel-2 offering 5-day coverage, and Landsat operating at 16-day intervals. These intervals mean that even with NRT processing, short-duration flash floods occurring between revisits may not be captured by satellite imagery.

Operational systems exhibit varying strategies to navigate the accuracy-timeliness trade-off. Fully automated SAR-based workflows, often employing thresholding or machine learning techniques, can generate flood extent maps within minutes to less than 12 hours after data availability [Wania et al. 2021], making them suitable for immediate emergency response and rapid situational awareness. These systems typically prioritize speed, accepting a degree of uncertainty. Conversely, semi-automated systems, such as those employed by UNITAR-UNOSAT, require 24 to 72 hours due to the integration of human-in-the-loop validation, thereby prioritizing higher accuracy and reliability over instantaneous delivery [Vanama et al. 2020]. This tiered approach is crucial for comprehensive disaster management, as summarized in Table 3 (Operational Classification of Flood Mapping Systems).

Model-based systems, such as the NASA Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS), offer an alternative by bypassing satellite acquisition delays entirely. These systems use precipitation-driven hydrological simulations to deliver updates every 3 hours, providing continuous NRT flood forecasts, albeit at a coarser spatial resolution (typically ~1 km) [Wu et al. 2012]. While not directly observational, they provide critical early warning and contextual information. Advanced operational strategies, such as pre-tasking satellite acquisitions using early warning systems (e.g., EFAS within CEMS), can further reduce response time by approximately half a day, underscoring the importance of integrating predictive and observational frameworks [Wania et al. 2021].

The Pareto frontier in Figure 2/F10 visually encapsulates this fundamental trade-off. Systems positioned towards the upper-left corner (high accuracy, low latency) represent optimal solutions. While deep learning models like U-Net can achieve exceptionally high accuracy, with overall accuracy (OA) up to 99% and F1 scores approaching 0.98 for SAR flood segmentation [Nemni et al. 2020; Andrew et al. 2023] (Thesis T3, CCS: HIGH), corpus-wide distributions indicate that such performance represents the upper extreme of reported flood segmentation accuracy (corpus F1 median: 80.0%, P75: 90.0%; corpus OA median: 89.7%, P75: 94.0%) (Figure 1/F1). The computational demands of these advanced models, especially for large-scale, NRT deployment, can shift them away from the immediate response end of the Pareto frontier, often requiring more processing time than simpler thresholding methods. Furthermore, inherent SAR limitations, such as classification errors up to 36% omission in densely vegetated floodplains and false positives from double-bounce effects in urban areas [Giustarini et al. 2013; Islam & Meng 2022] (Thesis T7, CCS: MEDIUM), mean that even highly accurate models must contend with sensor-specific ambiguities that can impact effective operational accuracy. Therefore, the choice of flood mapping system depends critically on the specific operational requirements, balancing the need for rapid situational awareness with the demand for precise and validated inundation extents.

# 8. Limitations and Uncertainty Sources

## 8. Limitations and Uncertainty Sources

Despite significant advancements in remote sensing for flood mapping, several inherent limitations and sources of uncertainty persist, impacting the accuracy, timeliness, and generalizability of derived products. These challenges are particularly pronounced in complex environments, such as densely vegetated floodplains, urban areas, and regions with limited ground truth data.

One primary source of uncertainty stems from the intrinsic characteristics of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data. While SAR offers all-weather, day-and-night imaging capabilities crucial for rapid response, its interaction with complex land cover introduces classification ambiguities. In densely vegetated floodplains, the canopy can attenuate the SAR signal, leading to significant omission errors where underlying floodwaters are undetected. Studies indicate that SAR classification error in such environments can reach up to 36% omission [Giustarini et al. 2013; Liang & Liu 2020]. Conversely, in urban areas, the "double-bounce" effect, where radar signals reflect off vertical structures (buildings) and then the ground, can be misinterpreted as standing water, resulting in false positives [Islam & Meng 2022]. These challenges necessitate sophisticated post-processing, such as vegetation masking or integration with ancillary data, to refine flood extent maps.

Optical remote sensing, while providing intuitive visual interpretation, is fundamentally constrained by atmospheric conditions. Cloud cover, prevalent during many flood events, severely limits data acquisition, often rendering optical imagery unusable for critical periods [Huang et al. 2018]. This limitation underscores the complementary role of SAR, but also highlights the need for robust multi-sensor fusion approaches to overcome individual sensor shortcomings [Anusha & Bharathi 2020].

Topographic data, particularly Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), represent another critical source of uncertainty, especially in low-relief floodplains. Global DEMs, such as SRTM, can introduce vertical errors of up to ±16 meters [Yamazaki et al. 2017]. In flat river basins, like the Dnipro, even minor vertical inaccuracies can propagate into substantial horizontal misclassifications of flood extent, significantly impacting the accuracy of inundation models and depth estimations [Hawker et al. 2022; Cohen et al. 2018]. The precision of flood depth estimation, crucial for damage assessment and humanitarian aid, is directly tied to the accuracy of the underlying DEM [Peter et al. 2020].

The increasing reliance on Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models, while promising high accuracy, introduces challenges related to model generalizability and transferability. Deep learning models, such as U-Net architectures, often exhibit reduced performance when applied to geographically distinct regions or unseen flood events, a phenomenon known as domain shift [Bentivoglio et al. 2022; Nemni et al. 2020]. This limitation necessitates extensive and diverse training datasets, or the application of domain adaptation techniques, to ensure robust performance across varied hydrological and environmental contexts. The co-occurrence heatmap in Figure 3 illustrates the strong pairing of certain methods with specific sensor types or regions, indirectly suggesting that models trained on particular datasets may not generalize universally. While overall accuracy (OA) metrics for DL models can reach high values (e.g., corpus median OA: 89.7%, P75: 94.0%), the variability shown in Figure 6 (F6_numericfact_accuracy_boxplot.png) underscores that such peak performance is not consistently achieved across all applications, particularly when models are deployed in novel environments.

Operational timeliness, critical for Near-Real-Time (NRT) flood response, also faces limitations. While cloud computing platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) have accelerated processing, significant delays can still occur. Evidence suggests that processing delays on such platforms can decouple satellite acquisition from actionable output by 30–70 hours [Vanama et al. 2020; Twele et al. 2016]. This latency, while improved over traditional desktop processing, can still hinder truly NRT applications, where decisions must be made within hours of an event's onset.

Furthermore, in regions affected by armed conflict, such as Eastern Europe and Ukraine, additional constraints exacerbate these limitations. Damage to infrastructure, displacement of populations, and restricted access can severely impede the collection of ground truth data, which is essential for model training and validation [Yailymov et al. 2025]. The lack of reliable ground observations makes it challenging to accurately assess the performance of remote sensing algorithms and quantify uncertainties, potentially leading to less reliable flood maps in critical humanitarian contexts. The cumulative effect of these uncertainties necessitates cautious interpretation of flood products and highlights the ongoing need for robust validation frameworks, especially in data-scarce and conflict-affected regions.

 and Ukrainian Case Studies

Satellite-based flood mapping in Eastern Europe holds particular significance due to the prevalence of transboundary river basins, complex mountainous terrain, and, in certain regions, conflict-affected areas where conventional *in situ* observations are sparse, fragmented, or inaccessible [Ekeu-wei & Blackburn 2018]. These conditions are acutely pronounced in Ukraine, where major flood-prone systems such as the Prut, Tisza, and Dnipro basins are characterized by cross-border data asymmetry, intricate topography, and, in some instances, infrastructure damage stemming from ongoing conflict. Under such challenging circumstances, satellite remote sensing offers a unique synoptic perspective, enabling the reconstruction of flood dynamics and the assessment of inundation impacts across politically and hydrologically complex territories. Figure F8 illustrates the locations of key case studies discussed in this section.

The Prut River basin, shared by Romania, Ukraine, and Moldova, exemplifies the confluence of transboundary governance challenges and the imperative for operational flood mapping. A study in this basin utilized Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and Sentinel-2 optical imagery to reconstruct daily flood wave progression, with recurrence probabilities estimated using Gumbel's distribution derived from upstream discharge data [Cîmpianu et al. 2021]. This entirely remote sensing-driven workflow was necessitated by the absence of harmonized cross-border LiDAR Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) across the riparian states. Despite these geospatial constraints, SAR-derived maximum inundation extents corresponding to peak flows of up to 2610 m³/s demonstrated over 95% spatial agreement with Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS) reference products [Cîmpianu et al. 2021]. This high level of agreement (Thesis T13, CCS: HIGH) underscores the robustness of satellite-only approaches for flood delineation, even in environments lacking integrated hydraulic-topographic datasets, positioning the Prut basin as a critical example of how remote sensing can compensate for fragmented institutional and geospatial infrastructures in Eastern Europe.

Further Ukrainian case studies reinforce the utility of satellite-based flood mapping. Following the catastrophic Kakhovka Dam disaster, a multi-sensor approach combining Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and Landsat-9 imagery, coupled with machine learning models including Random Forest (RF), U-Net, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), quantified an inundation area of 473 km² [Yailymov et al. 2025]. This analysis also delineated 1.67 thousand hectares of cropland and 1.85 thousand hectares of settlements affected by flooding. The associated irrigation-damage mapping achieved an overall accuracy (OA) of 90.4% (Thesis T14, CCS: HIGH) [Yailymov et al. 2025]. While this performance is commendable, it is important to contextualize it within corpus-wide distributions, where the median OA for flood mapping is 89.7% and the 75th percentile is 94.0% (NumericFact, metric.overall_accuracy). The Kakhovka case highlights the capacity of multi-source remote sensing and advanced machine learning to provide rapid, quantitative damage assessments in conflict zones, despite challenges such as mixed pixels and limited validation opportunities.

In Mukachevo, western Ukraine, a hybrid U-Net and Support Vector Machine (SVM) framework applied to Sentinel-2 imagery achieved an exceptionally high overall accuracy of 98.8% for urban and peri-urban flood delineation (Thesis T15, CCS: MEDIUM). This result, while representing the upper extreme of reported flood segmentation accuracy (corpus OA max: 100.0%, P75: 94.0%), illustrates the significant potential of deep learning for precise flood mapping in complex urban environments [Bentivoglio et al. 2022]. Historically, earlier neural network approaches also demonstrated feasibility in the region; for instance, a historical Tisza River application (spanning Ukraine and Hungary) employing ERS-2 SAR and Self-Organizing Kohonen Maps achieved an 85.40% classification accuracy [Kussul et al. 2008]. This indicates that semi-automated flood extraction in transboundary mountain environments has been a long-standing area of research and development.

Despite these successes, these case studies also reveal principal limitations inherent to flood mapping in the region. In the Prut basin, the persistent absence of shared high-resolution DEMs fundamentally constrains the transition from 2D satellite extent mapping to physically rigorous 3D hydrodynamic analysis, which requires accurate terrain representation [Cohen et al. 2018; Hawker et al. 2022]. In southern Ukraine, particularly in the context of the Kakhovka disaster, conflict conditions introduce complexities such as mixed pixels, the presence of shallow swampy water bodies, and severely restricted opportunities for ground validation. Furthermore, across densely forested floodplains, including comparable regional examples such as the Sava and Krka river systems, vegetation masking remains a major source of under-detection, with reported discrepancies in flood extent reaching up to 36% in heavily vegetated terrain [Gašparovič & Klobučar 2021]. These challenges underscore the need for robust algorithms capable of distinguishing water from vegetation and other confounding features [Huang et al. 2018].

Collectively, these findings suggest that Eastern Europe, and Ukraine in particular, should be considered not merely a peripheral application domain, but rather a critical testbed for next-generation flood mapping methodologies. These methods must integrate SAR, optical data, and advanced machine learning techniques with improved terrain information, all while operating under severe observational and infrastructural constraints [Destefanis et al. 2025]. The region's unique combination of transboundary hydrological systems, heterogeneous landscapes, and data scarcity necessitates innovative, adaptable, and resilient remote sensing solutions for effective flood risk management and disaster response.

### Region-Specific Limitations

Four constraints are structurally specific to Eastern European satellite flood monitoring:
1. **DEM fragmentation.** No freely available LiDAR DEM exists for the transboundary Ukrainian–Romanian–Moldovan corridor. Global DEMs (SRTM, MERIT, FABDEM) carry 5–16 m vertical uncertainty — sufficient to misplace flood boundary by kilometres in flat floodplains.
2. **Conflict-induced access restrictions.** Post-2022 field validation campaigns in southern Ukraine (Kakhovka, Dnipro delta) are infeasible, forcing sole reliance on satellite and modelled reference data.
3. **Reduced Sentinel-1 revisit (2021–2024).** Sentinel-1B failure in December 2021 increased effective revisit over Ukraine to ~12 days — critically reducing detection probability for flash floods of 1–5 day duration prior to Sentinel-1C launch in late 2024.
4. **Cross-border data fragmentation.** Operational CEMS activations are issued per administrative unit, while the physical flood system is transboundary. Analytical inconsistencies between national CEMS reports limit composite accuracy assessment.


# 10. Future Directions

## 10. Future Directions

Advancements in satellite-based flood mapping are critically dependent on overcoming the inherent limitations of single-sensor approaches and evolving towards more integrated, intelligent, and timely systems. The scientific consensus points towards multi-sensor data fusion as a foundational step, leveraging the complementary strengths of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and optical imagery [Anusha & Bharathi 2020]. While SAR offers all-weather, day-and-night acquisition capabilities crucial for near-real-time (NRT) monitoring, optical indices such as the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) provide superior spectral discrimination of water bodies [Xu 2006]. Future research must focus on developing unified multi-sensor fusion architectures that operate across spatial, temporal, and spectral domains, enabling dynamic flood mapping adaptable to diverse environmental conditions. Figure 7 illustrates the complex interplay of data sources and methods in such integrated frameworks, highlighting the central role of multi-sensor input from platforms like Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and Landsat.

A pivotal research direction involves the transition from purely data-driven models to physics-informed artificial intelligence (AI) frameworks. While Deep Learning (DL) models, such as U-Net, have demonstrated high accuracy in flood segmentation [Bentivoglio et al. 2022], they often lack physical interpretability and struggle with generalization across diverse geographic regions. Evidence suggests that integrating hydrological and hydraulic constraints—such as Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND), flow routing, and terrain gradients—into neural network architectures could significantly enhance model robustness and transferability (CCS: MEDIUM) [Bentivoglio et al. 2022, Amitrano et al. 2024, Guo et al. 2025]. Such integrated frameworks may improve flood extent delineation while supporting physically consistent estimation of hydraulic connectivity, flood depth, and inundation dynamics [Cohen et al. 2018, Cohen et al. 2019]. The sustained relevance of HAND as a terrain descriptor, as shown in Figure 4, underscores its potential for integration into next-generation DL models [Rennó et al. 2008].

Reducing latency in operational flood monitoring systems is another critical priority. Despite rapid SAR data acquisition (often within 10 minutes to 24 hours post-acquisition), end-to-end processing delays, particularly within cloud-based platforms, can extend total latency to 30–70 hours, thereby limiting the effectiveness of NRT applications [Wania et al. 2021]. Future systems should explore edge computing and in-orbit processing, where preliminary flood classification is performed directly on satellite platforms before data transmission (CCS: MEDIUM). Additionally, tighter integration between satellite tasking and Early Warning Systems (e.g., EFAS) can further reduce response times by enabling pre-acquisition of data prior to anticipated flood peaks, enhancing situational awareness during critical phases [Twele et al. 2016, Wu et al. 2012].

From a regional perspective, particularly in Eastern Europe and Ukraine, future research must prioritize the development of high-resolution, harmonized topographic datasets. The reliance on global Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) with significant vertical errors introduces substantial uncertainty in flood modeling, especially in low-relief floodplains such as the Dnipro basin [Hawker et al. 2022, Yamazaki et al. 2017]. Expanding access to LiDAR-quality Digital Terrain Models (DTMs), or developing robust correction frameworks for existing DEMs, is essential for improving both flood extent and depth estimation. Furthermore, integrating satellite-based observations with physically based hydrodynamic models (e.g., HEC-RAS 2D) represents a critical step toward operational flood forecasting systems that combine observational and predictive capabilities [Jafarzadegan et al. 2023].

Satellite-based flood mapping in Eastern Europe demonstrates particular value in transboundary basins, mountainous terrain, and conflict-affected regions, where in situ observations are sparse, fragmented, or inaccessible. In Ukraine, these conditions are especially pronounced, with flood-prone systems such as the Prut, Tisza, and Dnipro basins characterized by cross-border data asymmetry, complex topography, and, in some cases, war-related infrastructure damage. Under such conditions, satellite remote sensing provides a unique synoptic perspective for reconstructing flood dynamics and assessing inundation impacts across politically and hydrologically complex territories, as exemplified by the case studies presented in Figure 8.

The Prut River basin serves as a compelling case study, combining transboundary governance challenges with the need for operational flood mapping.  Conclusions

Flooding continues to be a pervasive and escalating natural hazard globally, with its impacts amplified by climate change, rapid urbanization, and the increasing vulnerability of exposed populations. In Eastern Europe and Ukraine, this risk is further exacerbated by complex transboundary river systems, diverse terrain, and recent catastrophic hydrological events, such as the 2020 Carpathian floods and the 2023 Kakhovka Dam breach. Satellite remote sensing has emerged as an indispensable tool for flood inundation mapping, offering rapid, spatially continuous, and objective observations, particularly in regions where ground-based monitoring infrastructure is limited or compromised [Klemas 2015; Kuntla 2020].

This review critically evaluated the performance, operational timeliness, and practical applicability of satellite-based flood mapping methodologies, encompassing optical, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), and advanced machine learning/deep learning (ML/DL) approaches. Optical methods, notably those employing the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI), consistently achieve high classification accuracies, often exceeding 93% Overall Accuracy (OA) under clear-sky conditions in mixed land-cover environments [Xu 2006; Zhang et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2018]. However, their utility is fundamentally constrained by cloud cover, which frequently coincides with peak flood events. In contrast, SAR-based techniques, predominantly utilizing Sentinel-1 data, provide all-weather, day-and-night monitoring capabilities, crucial for rapid response [Schumann & Moller 2015; Amitrano et al. 2024]. Supervised classifiers like Random Forest, when applied to Sentinel-1 VH/VV polarizations, frequently yield OA values above 94% and Cohen's kappa coefficients between 0.75 and 0.80 for flood delineation [Islam & Meng 2022; Toma et al. 2024].

Deep learning architectures, particularly U-Net-based semantic segmentation models, represent the current state-of-the-art in flood mapping, with some studies reporting exceptional performance, including OA values approaching 99% and F1-scores up to 0.98 for SAR flood segmentation [Nemni et al. 2020; Andrew et al. 2023; Bentivoglio et al. 2022]. However, it is crucial to contextualize these peak performances against corpus-wide accuracy distributions. While such values represent the upper extreme under optimized conditions, the median F1-score across the literature is approximately 80.0% (P75: 90.0%), and the median OA is 89.7% (P75: 94.0%). This highlights that typical operational performance may be lower than benchmark results. Furthermore, the implementation of advanced ML/DL systems often introduces increased computational demands, processing latency, and challenges related to model transferability across diverse geographical and hydrological contexts [Mateo-Garcia et al. 2021].

A persistent trade-off exists between mapping accuracy and operational timeliness. High-precision methods often necessitate extensive preprocessing, large volumes of labeled training data, or expert validation, thereby increasing latency. Conversely, rapid-response systems prioritize timely situational awareness, often accepting a degree of increased uncertainty, particularly in complex urban or densely vegetated areas [Giustarini et al. 2013; Gašparovič & Klobučar 2021]. Operational frameworks, such as the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS), exemplify the importance of integrating automated SAR workflows with multi-sensor data fusion to achieve an optimal balance between speed and reliability. Sentinel-1 data, for instance, enables near-real-time (NRT) flood map delivery within 10 minutes to 24 hours of acquisition, with CEMS SAR products typically delivered within 6.4 hours, significantly faster than optical products (39.5 hours) [Twele et al. 2016; Wania et al. 2021]. The high confidence in these benchmark and operational claims is visually summarized in Figure F_CCS.

Case studies from Eastern Europe underscore the critical role of satellite remote sensing in data-sparse and transboundary regions. For instance, SAR-only approaches combining Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data with hydrological recurrence analysis in the Prut River basin achieved over 95% spatial agreement with CEMS reference data, even in the absence of cross-border LiDAR DEMs [Cîmpianu et al. 2021]. Similarly, the Kakhovka Dam disaster in Ukraine demonstrated the capability of integrated ML approaches (Random Forest, U-Net, MLP) on multi-sensor data (Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, Landsat-9) to map inundated irrigation areas with 90.4% OA, identifying approximately 473 km² of flooded land [Yailymov et al. 2025]. These examples confirm the robustness of satellite-based methods in challenging environments.

In conclusion, no single satellite-based methodology is universally optimal for flood mapping. Instead, a tiered, integrated multi-sensor framework combining the all-weather capabilities of SAR, the spectral richness of optical imagery, and high-resolution topographic information offers the most robust and adaptable solution for operational flood monitoring and disaster response [Anusha & Bharathi 2020; Destefanis et al. 2025]. Future research should prioritize the development of physics-informed machine learning models, the integration of improved topographic datasets (e.g., those accounting for vegetation and buildings [Hawker et al. 2022]), and further reductions in processing latency to support next-generation near-real-time flood mapping systems, particularly in high-risk and conflict-affected environments such as Ukraine.
